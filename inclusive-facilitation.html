Facilitation and Capacity Building

A reader who works through this resource can scope a facilitation or capacity building engagement — its entry point, its participants, its depth — and judge whether what it produces will still be running after the facilitator leaves.

Who this is for. Facilitators, learning designers, faculty developers, and the people who commission facilitation and decide what happens to what it surfaces. It assumes no prior background in accessibility or facilitation theory and defines terms as they arise. The service page is at accessiblelearninglabs.com/facilitation-and-capacity-building.

Section one of seven

Foundations

Inclusive facilitation is a professional stance rather than a set of techniques. It rests on four knowledge domains, each necessary and none sufficient on its own.

The domains are present simultaneously in any session, in proportions that depend on what the work requires. A technically skilled facilitator who has not examined their own position will reproduce the dynamics they intend to interrupt. A capacity builder with sophisticated organisational knowledge and limited relational skill produces systems that read as inclusive and are experienced as alienating.

Technical

Framework proficiency

Fluency in Universal Design for Learning, equity and inclusion frameworks, and the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines. What matters is translating them into concrete design decisions — the structure of a session, the format of material, the pacing, how feedback is collected — rather than treating them as compliance checklists.

Relational

Collaborative and participatory design

Creating the conditions in which participants’ knowledge, including their knowledge of the barriers they encounter, shapes the work. Consultation asks people to react to decisions already made; co-design involves them in making the decisions. In higher education this is formalised as students as partners.

Structural

Systemic capacity building

Understanding organisational change well enough to know where an intervention will take hold and where it will be absorbed without effect. This is what turns facilitation from a service delivered to an organisation into something the organisation can do for itself.

Reflective

Reflective practice and positionality

Critical examination of one’s own biases, power and identity. Not because a facilitator must have resolved their position before working, but because unexamined position shows up in session design in ways that exclude particular participants without the facilitator noticing. This domain keeps the other three honest.

Facilitation and instruction are different work

An instructor transfers knowledge into a well-designed structure. A facilitator creates the conditions in which knowledge emerges, so that people holding different positions and different relationships to power can think together productively. Inclusive facilitation adds one further requirement: sustained attention to who is being excluded from that process, and the willingness to name exclusion while it is happening rather than after.

Accommodating and inclusive by design are different postures

Facilitation that accommodates responds to difference once it presents as a problem, adjusting when a participant says they cannot access something. Facilitation that is inclusive by design anticipates variability, builds in more than one way to take part, and treats belonging as the starting condition rather than something to be managed. The distinction determines whether a participant encounters access or a gesture toward it.

The thread

Three lenses, one session. WCAG examines the artefact — slides, documents, captions, platform. Universal Design for Learning examines whether the experience is designed for variability. Inclusive design examines whether the organisation’s processes reduce barriers across the whole participant journey. The three answer different questions, and a facilitator who holds only the first will produce sessions that are technically accessible and structurally unchanged.

Section two of seven

Process

Six stages, in the order they are carried out. The arc runs from reading a brief to leaving behind capacity that does not depend on the facilitator.

Depth and duration vary with the engagement. Stages one and two determine whether the rest of the work is aimed at the actual problem, and stage six determines whether any of it survives. The stages in between are where most of the visible work happens and where most engagements are judged.

1 Read the brief, and what it leaves unstated A brief describes a chosen response; the work starts with the problem +

A commissioned brief already contains a decision. Someone has determined what the problem is and what form the response should take. Before designing to it, the facilitator establishes who made that determination, what made it a priority now, and whose presence or absence in the room will shape what can be said.

Briefs that follow a complaint, an audit finding or a regulatory deadline carry a specific risk: participants arrive with their defences already engaged, and content that would land in other conditions does not land in these. Reading that before designing is what separates a series that is attended from one that changes something.

This stage produces
  • A statement of the problem the brief responds to, distinct from the brief
  • The commissioning history: who decided, and what made it urgent now
  • An assessment of the conditions participants will arrive in
  • Agreement on what is in scope beyond the stated deliverable
2 Establish context with the people affected Starting from existing capacity rather than a predefined solution +

Context is established through conversations across the organisation: equity and disability services, unit or department leads, and the participants themselves. What is already working matters as much as what is not, because capacity building starts from existing capacity. This is the principle underlying the UNESCO IESALC capacity-building methodology, which begins with collaborative analysis of an institution’s actual conditions rather than with a solution decided in advance.

The people closest to a barrier are the most reliable guide to it. Capacity building designed without input from those most affected by current practice tends to produce changes that are visible in documentation and marginal in experience.

This stage produces
  • A shared account of current practice, including what is working
  • Identification of existing internal capacity and where it sits
  • Barriers documented in the words of the people who encounter them
  • Institutional constraints: governance, approval pathways, procurement
3 Design the arc Making the form of the facilitation carry its content +

A series is designed as a sequence with a shape rather than a set of topics distributed across dates. The opening establishes the conditions under which people can think. The middle raises the difficulty. The later sessions convert what has surfaced into something the organisation can act on.

The consequential design decision is where the facilitator locates the participants. A session that opens by asking people to examine their own practice positions them as the problem. A session that opens from the shared experience of being constrained by someone else’s design positions everyone in the same place first. The form of the facilitation has to enact its content, or the delivery contradicts the material.

This stage produces
  • A session sequence with a stated arc and the rationale behind it
  • Material meeting WCAG, with more than one way to take part built in
  • Participation structures that do not reward speaking first or fastest
  • A plan for what happens when the room becomes difficult
4 Facilitate, including the difficult parts Holding what surfaces rather than resolving it early +

Inclusive facilitation is not the smooth delivery of content. Its distinguishing skill is noticing power operating in the room — who speaks, whose contributions are built on, whose are passed over — and working with it rather than around it.

When a participant recognises that practice they have held for years may have been excluding people, reassurance is the wrong response. It is inaccurate, and it removes the most useful thing that has happened. Naming what is occurring and letting the room stay with it is harder and more productive. Multipartial facilitation — supporting multiple perspectives simultaneously rather than claiming neutrality — is the term the practice literature uses for this stance.

This stage produces
  • Documented observations of participation and group dynamics
  • Barriers named by participants during sessions rather than afterwards
  • Adjustments made within the series in response to what surfaced
5 Convert what surfaced into structural work Following the room into governance +

Facilitation regularly surfaces obstacles no individual can resolve: approval pathways, procurement practice, committee resistance, workload allocation. When it does, the useful move is to follow it rather than return to the agenda. A structural barrier is more tractable than a knowledge gap once it has been named and attributed.

Co-design sessions turn this into output. Participants working on their own material identify the specific point at which the institution stops them, and the group produces a proposal addressed to whoever controls that point. Work of this kind is frequently outside the brief and inside the scope.

This stage produces
  • A record of structural barriers, each attributed to a specific process or body
  • Proposals addressed to the people with authority over them
  • A distinction between what the group can change and what it must escalate
6 Transfer capacity and hand off Building what continues after the facilitator leaves +

An organisation that relies on external facilitation for its inclusion work remains dependent on it. Capacity building identifies internal practitioners, develops them through modelling and structured practice, and leaves behind what they need to work without support: facilitation guides, accessible templates, shared vocabulary, and a community of practice with somewhere to meet.

The test of an engagement is not what participants report at the close. It is whether the practice is still running two years later, and whether it survives the departure of the people who introduced it.

This stage produces
  • Named internal facilitators with a development path
  • Facilitation guides, accessible templates and shared reference material
  • Recommendations for governance, procurement and professional development
  • An approach to evaluating the practice over time
Section three of seven

Decisions

Six choices that determine what an engagement can produce. Each can be answered more than one way, and each answer has a cost.

These are settled at the outset and constrain everything afterwards. Naming them in advance lets the commissioning organisation recognise later whether it received the engagement it needed.

Scope

What is actually being built?

A session or series delivers facilitated events with inclusive design embedded throughout, and produces the clearest short-term result. Facilitation development builds internal practitioners through modelling, mentoring and structured practice, and produces the slowest visible result and the most durable one. Organisational capacity building develops the structures, shared language and leadership that sustain the practice institutionally. A facilitation audit reviews what already exists. Each scope answers a different question, and an organisation that commissions the first while needing the third will get good sessions and no change.

Entry point

Where does the engagement begin?

Proactive design builds inclusive practice into new programmes before launch, and is where the work costs least. Responsive redesign reworks existing approaches against identified gaps. Crisis response supports an organisation navigating a specific incident or complaint, and has to address both the immediate situation and the design problem underneath it. Audit-first reviews before recommending, which is the most defensible start when the design history is unclear. Crisis is the most common entry point and the hardest, because participants arrive with their defences already engaged.

Participation

Whose knowledge shapes the work?

Facilitators and learning designers will develop and deliver what is built. Participants and learners are the most reliable source of information about where the design creates friction. Institutional leaders hold authority over the structural and governance decisions that determine whether the practice can scale. Equity specialists bring expertise in the specific dimensions most relevant to the context. Leaving any of the four out is a defensible decision; leaving them out without noticing is how engagements produce changes that are visible on paper and marginal in experience.

Depth and duration

How thorough does the engagement need to be?

A rapid assessment identifies the main gaps efficiently and is useful for prioritisation, provided its findings are treated as a starting point rather than a complete picture. A full engagement develops practice across all four domains. An ongoing partnership embeds the work in the organisation’s own development cycle. Depth is usually constrained by budget, but the constraint that decides the outcome is more often calendar access to the people who need to be in the room.

Focus

Where is the engagement primarily directed?

Session design addresses materials, pacing, participation structures and digital content. Facilitation practice addresses how facilitators read and respond to group dynamics and power. Systems and governance address institutional policy, approval pathways and procurement. These are different bodies of work with different timelines, and an engagement that promises all three inside one budget will deliver the first.

Audience

Who has to act on what the engagement produces?

Practitioner documentation gives facilitators and designers specific, reproducible detail. A strategic summary gives leadership and governance the prioritisation and resourcing context they need. Capacity building distributes the knowledge through the engagement itself rather than through a document at the end. Settling the audience before the work begins changes what is gathered along the way, not only how it is presented.

Section four of seven

Competencies

What a person doing this work must be able to do. Each capability maps onto a stage of the process above and draws on one or more of the four domains.

These are capabilities rather than dispositions, so each can be observed in the work produced. They are drawn from the competency literature named in Sources and from the process set out in Process.

Read power in a room

Notices who speaks, whose contributions are built on and whose are passed over, and can describe the pattern specifically enough to change the structure that produced it rather than commenting on it.

Hold difficulty without resolving it early

Names what is happening when a session becomes uncomfortable and lets the room stay with it, instead of reassuring, moving on, or filling the silence. Produces sessions where disagreement surfaces rather than persisting unspoken.

Translate frameworks into design decisions

Converts UDL, equity frameworks and WCAG into the specific structure of a session: its pacing, its materials, its participation formats, and how feedback is collected. Can explain to an institutional audience what a framework requires, not only what it permits.

Produce facilitation material that meets WCAG

Captions media, structures documents and slides so they can be navigated by keyboard and screen reader, and can tell a genuine defect from an automated false positive.

Run co-design rather than consultation

Designs sessions in which participants hold real decisions, under conditions of informed consent and fair compensation, and can navigate disagreement without steering the group toward the consensus that is easiest to report.

State and work from one’s own positionality

Can articulate how their own identity and institutional position shape what they notice and what they miss, and adjusts session design accordingly rather than treating their own experience of a space as the neutral case.

Locate a barrier in a governance process

Traces an obstacle raised in a session to the specific committee, policy, contract or workflow that produces it, and writes a proposal addressed to whoever controls that point.

Develop other facilitators

Builds internal capability through modelling, structured practice and feedback, and leaves behind guides and templates specific enough to be used without the original facilitator present.

Section five of seven

Outcomes

What an engagement produces while it is running, and what tends to follow over a longer period. The two carry different evidential weight.

Outcomes within the engagement are observable at the time and can be verified by someone who was in the room. Longer-term change is what the descriptive literature and practice reports suggest tends to follow, and is stated here with the hedging that evidence supports.

Within the engagement

  • Power dynamics become nameableIn most professional learning environments, power shapes who speaks and whose contributions are built on while remaining unnamed. Facilitation that surfaces it makes it something the group can work with rather than something individuals absorb privately.
  • Shared language developsAn organisation that cannot describe a problem specifically cannot address it. A well-designed series produces vocabulary precise enough for colleagues to disagree about the right thing.
  • Participation is redistributedParticipation changes in distribution and in kind, not only in volume. Previously peripheral contributors begin to contribute substantively, and suppressed disagreement surfaces, which is uncomfortable and useful.
  • Structural barriers are identified and attributedApproval pathways, procurement practice, committee resistance and workload allocation are named, and each is attached to the body or process that controls it, which is what makes them addressable.

Over time

  • Inclusion becomes a design questionOrganisations that have invested in capacity building tend to ask a different question about a new learning event: not whether it is compliant, but who it is designed for and who it is not.
  • Internal facilitators existWhere facilitation development was part of the scope, the organisation has named people who can run inclusive sessions without external support, which is the difference between a capability and a contract.
  • Structural supports outlast individualsAccessible templates, facilitation guides, governance pathways and communities of practice are what allow the work to continue when the people who introduced it move on. Whether this has happened is the clearest available indicator that capacity was built rather than borrowed.
  • Design features are used more widely than intendedClearer structure, multiple ways to take part, plain language and consistent navigation are used by participants who reported no access need and were not the intended beneficiaries.
Limits

What this work does not do, and what the evidence does not yet show. Facilitation does not resolve institutional resistance. It makes resistance visible and gives the people who are ready to move a shared language for doing so, which is a slower and more accurate account than most briefs allow for. It also cannot supply authority: where nobody in the room controls the governance process a barrier sits in, the engagement will document a barrier that persists. On evidence, the meta-analytic base concerns Universal Design for Learning as a design framework rather than facilitation practice, and even there the findings are qualified — one meta-analysis reports improvement to the learning process while stating that effects on educational outcomes have not been demonstrated. The capacity building literature cited here is methodological and descriptive rather than experimental. These are reasons to adopt the approach and to describe its effects carefully.

Section six of seven

Reflection

Four questions about current practice, and four descriptions of what different patterns of answer usually indicate.

Choose the option closest to the current situation rather than the intended one. The descriptions that follow address the situation, not the people working within it.

How does your organisation currently approach facilitation design?

Consider what happens routinely rather than what happened once.

How are participants’ perspectives on access and inclusion gathered?

This asks about structured mechanisms, not about handling complaints.

To what extent does reflective practice on positionality and power feature in the work?

This asks about structured, shared practice rather than private reflection.

Where does capacity for inclusive facilitation currently sit?

Consider what would happen if the person who does most of this work left.

No structured approach yet

In this pattern, the quality of a session depends on who happens to be running it, and access issues reach the organisation as individual requests rather than as design information. That reflects the absence of a process rather than the priorities of the people doing the work. The most useful next step is to take one recurring session and document how it is currently designed, because a written account of current practice is what makes the gaps visible enough to discuss.

Practice is reactive

Adjustments are being made and they do help the participants who ask, but they arrive after the fact and place the work of asking on the people already facing the barrier. This is the most common position. The next step is to look at the adjustments already made across recent sessions and ask which of them describe the same barrier, then build that one into the default design.

Practice is established in places

Method exists and produces good sessions where it is applied, so the constraint is usually reach and durability rather than skill. The next step is to move one practice out of an individual’s habit and into shared infrastructure — an accessible session template, or a facilitation guide with the participation structures written down — so that it applies without anyone having to remember it.

Capacity is institutional

The practice is held in governance and professional development rather than in particular people, and it would survive their departure. The remaining work is usually in reach and in renewal: new staff, new platforms and new programmes each introduce the possibility of reverting. The next step is to build inclusive facilitation into how new facilitators are onboarded, so that the standard is transmitted rather than assumed.

Capp, M. J. "The effectiveness of universal design for learning: a meta-analysis of literature between 2013 and 2016." International Journal of Inclusive Education, 21(8), 791–807, 2017. Reports that UDL improves the learning process for all students while stating that its impact on educational outcomes has not been demonstrated. This is the basis of the hedging in Outcomes.
