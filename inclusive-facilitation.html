Inclusive Facilitation and Capacity Building

A guide to the knowledge foundations, competency domains, practice, and organisational outcomes that characterise effective inclusive facilitation in higher education, the public sector, and research institutions.

ℹ️ This resource is for learning designers, facilitators, faculty, and organisational leaders. Use the tabs to explore at whatever depth is useful to you.

What Inclusive Facilitation Actually Is

Inclusive facilitation is not a set of techniques applied to a session. It is a professional stance — shaped by how the facilitator understands power, identity, and whose knowledge is treated as legitimate. It draws on four interconnected knowledge domains, each of which is necessary and none of which is sufficient alone.

🧰

Technical & Framework Proficiency

Fluency in Universal Design for Learning (UDL), Diversity, Equity, Inclusion, and Justice (DEIJ) frameworks, and Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG). This means translating frameworks into concrete design decisions — not treating them as compliance checklists.

🤝

Collaborative & Participatory Design

Moving beyond expert-led facilitation toward co-creation — treating participants' knowledge of the barriers they face as central to the design process. In higher education, this includes "students as partners" models; across sectors, it means genuine co-design rather than consultation.

🏛️

Systemic Capacity Building

Understanding organisational change well enough to move beyond session-level improvements toward structural transformation. Effective capacity building develops shared knowledge, language, and leadership that persists when external facilitation support ends.

🪞

Reflective Practice & Positionality

Critical examination of one's own biases, power, and identity — not because facilitators must have resolved their positioning, but because unexamined positioning tends to reproduce the power dynamics it intends to address. This domain keeps the other three honest.

A practical rule: Use WCAG to audit the artefact. Use UDL to evaluate whether the learning experience is designed for variability. Use inclusive design principles to ask whether the organisation's processes and culture reduce barriers across the whole participant journey. Effective facilitation holds all three simultaneously.

Facilitation versus Instruction

A skilled instructor transfers knowledge into a well-designed structure. A skilled facilitator holds space for knowledge to emerge — enabling people with different positions, perspectives, and relationships to power to think together productively. Inclusive facilitation adds a further dimension: active attention to who is being excluded from that process, and the willingness to name and address exclusion as it unfolds.

Accommodating versus Inclusive by Design

Facilitation that is accommodating responds to difference when it presents as a problem — adjusting when a participant raises their hand to say they cannot access something. Facilitation that is inclusive by design anticipates variability, builds in multiple ways to participate, and treats belonging as the starting point rather than a problem to be managed. The distinction is not merely pedagogical: it determines whether marginalised participants experience genuine access or a gesture toward it.

Note: Critical reflection on one's own biases, power, and identity is not a supplementary competency. It is the foundation on which every other facilitation skill rests.

A Multi-Dimensional Competency Framework

No domain is sufficient alone. A technically skilled facilitator who has not examined their own positionality will reproduce the power dynamics they intend to address. A capacity builder with sophisticated organisational knowledge but limited relational skill produces systems that look inclusive on paper and feel alienating in practice. Integration across all four domains is what distinguishes competent inclusive facilitation. Select any domain to explore it.

Mastery of Universal Design for Learning (UDL) and DEIJ frameworks is the precondition for designing environments that are inherently adaptable — where flexibility is built in from the outset rather than added as a retrospective accommodation.

In practice, this means translating frameworks into concrete design decisions: the structure of a workshop, the format of materials, the pacing of a session, and how feedback is collected. It also means understanding technical standards such as WCAG — not as a compliance checklist, but as a design language that defines what genuine digital accessibility requires across sensory, cognitive, and motor profiles.

Practitioners proficient in these frameworks can articulate not just what they are doing but why — and can help institutions understand what the frameworks demand, not only what they permit.

What this enables

  • Sessions and materials designed for variability from the outset, not retrofitted
  • Digital content meeting accessibility standards across all formats — slides, documents, recorded materials
  • The ability to explain framework decisions to institutional stakeholders in terms they can act on

Collaborative and participatory design inverts the expert-led model: the facilitator creates conditions in which participants' knowledge — including their knowledge of the barriers they face — surfaces and shapes the work. The distinction between consultation and co-design matters. Consultation asks people to react to decisions already made. Co-design involves them in making the decisions — and produces fundamentally different outcomes.

In higher education, this appears as "students as partners" — a model grounded in respect, reciprocity, and shared responsibility, in which students are active contributors to course and session design rather than passive recipients. As Cook-Sather, Bovill, and Felten describe it, this is a collaborative, reciprocal process through which all participants contribute to educational outcomes.

This requires facilitation skill beyond process management: holding space for historically excluded voices, surfacing and navigating disagreement productively, and resisting the pull toward false consensus that produces inclusion in name only.

What this enables

  • Learning environments where participation is genuinely distributed, not performed
  • Design processes that surface barriers expert-led approaches systematically miss
  • A relationship between facilitators and participants that builds trust and shared ownership over time
Note: The UNESCO IESALC methodology for capacity building in higher education formalises this approach: beginning with collaborative analysis of each institution's actual context and challenges rather than imposing predefined solutions.

Individual facilitation skill, however excellent, does not change organisations. What changes organisations is the gradual accumulation of shared knowledge, structural supports, and leadership that makes inclusive practice possible beyond any single facilitator or session. This is capacity building: transforming facilitation from a service delivered to an organisation into something the organisation develops the ability to do for itself.

Practitioners in this domain understand organisational change well enough to know where interventions are likely to take hold and where they will be absorbed without effect. They can assess institutional climate, mobilise knowledge across departments, build coalitions, and help institutions develop the leadership structures that sustain inclusive practice when external support ends.

This is particularly important in large institutions, where equity efforts often succeed in pockets and fail to scale — not because the facilitation was poor, but because the organisational conditions for diffusion were never built.

What this enables

  • A shift from session-level improvements to systemic transformation
  • Shared language, frameworks, and tools that persist after an engagement ends
  • Leadership capacity to sustain inclusive practice through institutional change

Reflective practice keeps the other three domains honest. Without it, framework proficiency becomes dogma; collaborative design becomes a performance that replicates existing power dynamics; and capacity building becomes an imposition of one organisation's values on another.

Critical reflection on one's own biases, power, and identity is foundational — not because facilitators must have resolved their positioning before they work, but because unexamined positioning tends to show up in session design in ways that exclude particular participants without the facilitator's awareness. What feels like a neutral, welcoming space to the facilitator may feel very different to participants accustomed to being accommodated rather than assumed.

This domain also encompasses the capacity to navigate institutional resistance to change — working productively with the reality that equity in practice means changing arrangements that benefit those who currently hold institutional advantage.

What this enables

  • The ability to notice and adjust when facilitation is inadvertently excluding or marginalising
  • A grounded approach to navigating resistance to change in institutional contexts
  • Facilitation that models the reflective practice it aims to build in others
How the domains work together: In any given session, all four domains are present simultaneously — in different proportions depending on what the work requires. Understanding which domains are most heavily drawn on helps facilitators and organisations clarify what they are developing and where the gaps lie.

An Illustrated Scenario

The following composite scenario is drawn from patterns that appear consistently across facilitation work in higher education institutions, public sector organisations, and research bodies. The context is directly recognisable to those working in academic settings; the dynamics it surfaces — defensiveness, governance barriers, and the gap between stated commitment and structural readiness — are common across all three sectors.

The brief — and what it contained

A mid-sized university faculty commissioned a professional learning series for academic staff on inclusive assessment: six workshops, ninety minutes each, focused on Universal Design for Learning and its application to assessment practice.

The facilitation team's first step was to sit with the brief rather than design immediately to it. Briefs of this kind carry significant unstated content: who decided this was the entry point, what had made it a priority now, and whose presence or absence in the room would shape what could be said.

What the intake conversations surfaced

Conversations with the faculty's equity office, department chairs, and disability services coordinators revealed the immediate trigger: a formal complaint from a student cohort — primarily students with disabilities and students whose first language was not English — about assessment practices in required courses. The complaint had been resolved through individual accommodation processes, but those processes had been slow and inconsistently applied.

The faculty leadership had responded by commissioning the professional development series — a genuine attempt to address a systemic problem. Without careful facilitation design, however, it risked failing in a specific way: staff arriving to a workshop on inclusive assessment in the wake of a complaint would arrive with defences activated. The facilitation challenge was not content — the content of inclusive assessment practice is well established. The challenge was designing a learning environment in which people who felt implicated could genuinely engage.

Designing the arc

The series was redesigned from a content-delivery model to a facilitated inquiry model. The first workshop did not begin with UDL. It began with a question: When have you been in a learning environment where the conditions made it difficult for you to do your best thinking? Participants wrote in silence for three minutes before any discussion. This was a designed intervention: locating the facilitator and the faculty in the same position — people who know what it is to be constrained by design — before anyone was asked to examine their own design decisions.

This is what it means for the form of facilitation to enact its content.

The moment the facilitation became uncomfortable

In the third workshop, participants were asked to analyse one of their existing assessments: what does it actually measure? Is it measuring the stated learning outcome, or something else — facility with a particular format, performance under time pressure, academic writing in a second language?

One senior faculty member pushed back directly: "I've been designing this course for fifteen years. Are you telling me it's been excluding students the whole time?"

The facilitator did not reassure. Reassurance at that moment would have been dishonest and would have undermined both the faculty member and the purpose of the workshop. Instead, the facilitator named what was happening and invited the room to stay with the discomfort rather than resolve it prematurely. The silence that followed was held, not filled. Several participants spoke into it, including one who said: "I had the same feeling — and I think the honest answer is: probably yes, probably not intentionally, and that's actually the most important thing to understand."

This is what inclusive facilitation looks like in practice: not the smooth delivery of content, but the skilled holding of difficulty.

What the co-design session produced

The fifth workshop was a co-design session: faculty working in small groups to redesign one of their own assessments. One group surfaced the institution's assessment governance: faculty wanting to redesign their assessments were discovering that many proposed formats would require approval processes from committees historically resistant to assessment innovation.

The facilitators stayed with what the room had surfaced — because what had emerged was exactly the information that the structural domain requires. The barrier was not knowledge or will. The barrier was governance. And governance is addressable.

The conversation produced a set of recommendations the faculty group agreed to bring to the next curriculum committee, including a proposal for a streamlined approval pathway for UDL-grounded assessment redesigns. None of this had been in the brief. All of it was within scope.

What did not change

Not everything shifted. Several initially resistant faculty remained sceptical at the close. The governance barriers surfaced in the co-design session were real, and the proposal to the curriculum committee faced a longer road than participants had hoped.

Inclusive facilitation does not resolve institutional resistance. It creates conditions in which resistance becomes visible — and in which those ready to move can do so with shared language and commitment. That is a slower and more accurate account of what facilitation can achieve than most institutional briefs allow for.

What this illustrates: The most significant outcomes of this series were unplanned. They emerged from a design responsive enough to follow what the room surfaced, and from facilitation skilled enough to hold the difficulty that surfacing brought.

What Changes — and When

Inclusive facilitation creates change at two horizons. Shorter-term outcomes are tangible and often visible within a series or engagement. The longer-term shift is structural — and determines whether the work endures or whether the organisation reverts to its prior equilibrium once external support ends.

Shorter Term

What becomes visible and addressable

✓

Power dynamics become legible

In most professional learning environments, power dynamics shape who speaks and whose contributions are built on — but remain unnamed. Inclusive facilitation makes them visible enough to be worked with.

✓

Shared language develops

Organisations that cannot talk about a problem specifically cannot fix it. A well-designed facilitation series produces shared vocabulary — terms and frames that enable colleagues to discuss inclusion and equity precisely enough to act.

✓

Participation is redistributed

When facilitation is well designed, participation changes in quality and distribution, not just volume. Previously peripheral contributors begin to contribute substantively — and suppressed disagreement surfaces, which environments that can hold genuine dissent are more durable for.

✓

Governance barriers are identified

Structured facilitation surfaces the structural obstacles — procurement practices, approval pathways, committee resistance — that no individual designer or facilitator can address alone.

Longer Term

What becomes embedded in practice

→

Inclusion becomes a design question

Organisations that have invested in genuine capacity building ask different questions about new learning events: not "are we compliant?" but "who is this designed for, and who is not?" This shift — from reactive to proactive — is the deepest form of institutional change.

→

Internal facilitators are developed

Organisations that rely entirely on external facilitation for their inclusion work remain in a state of dependency. Meaningful capacity building identifies and develops internal practitioners who can facilitate inclusive environments and sustain the work across time.

→

Structural supports outlast individuals

The most important indicator of successful capacity building is whether inclusive practice continues when the people who built it leave. Accessible templates, facilitation guides, governance pathways, and communities of practice are what create that continuity.

→

All participants benefit

Better-structured environments, clearer language, flexible formats, and consistent navigation improve the experience for every participant — not only those from marginalised groups.

On evidence and outcomes: Research consistently demonstrates that accessible, inclusive facilitation supports retention, participation, and equity beyond compliance. The strongest institutional case for investment in this work is not the legal argument alone — it is the evidence that better-designed facilitation produces better outcomes for more participants.

What Shapes an Engagement

No two engagements look the same. The decisions an organisation makes about scope, entry point, and who is at the table determine what kind of work is possible and what it can realistically produce. Understanding these dimensions helps set honest expectations — and make better use of the facilitation and capacity building process.

🎯

Scope

What is actually being built?

Session or series — facilitation of one or more learning events with inclusive design embedded throughout

Facilitation development — building internal practitioners' capacity through modelling, mentoring, and structured practice

Organisational capacity building — developing the structures, shared language, and leadership that sustain inclusive practice institutionally

Facilitation audit — reviewing existing facilitation practices for accessibility and equity, with recommendations for change

🚪

Entry Point

Where does the engagement begin?

Proactive design — building inclusive practice into new programmes before they launch. The most effective entry point

Responsive redesign — reworking existing approaches in light of identified gaps or participant feedback

Crisis response — supporting organisations navigating a specific incident or complaint, addressing both the immediate situation and the underlying design problems

Audit-first — reviewing what currently exists before recommending change; the most responsible starting point when the design history is unclear

👥

Who Is at the Table

Whose knowledge shapes the work?

Facilitators and learning designers — those who will develop and deliver facilitated experiences

Participants and learners — the most reliable source of information about where design creates friction

Institutional leaders — those with authority over structural and governance decisions that determine whether inclusive practice can scale

Equity and inclusion specialists — those with expertise in the specific equity dimensions most relevant to the institutional context

📄

Reporting & Use of Findings

Who needs to act, and how?

Practitioner documentation — specific findings for facilitators and designers

Strategic summary — for leadership, governance, and procurement decisions

Capacity building — sharing knowledge across the team through the engagement process itself

⏱

Depth and Duration

How thorough does the engagement need to be?

Rapid assessment — key gaps identified efficiently for prioritisation, understanding findings as a starting point rather than a complete picture

Full engagement — comprehensive development across all four competency domains

Ongoing partnership — iterative, embedded in the organisation's development cycle over time

🏗️

Focus Area

Where is the engagement primarily directed?

Session design — materials, pacing, participation structures, and digital content

Facilitation practice — how facilitators read and respond to group dynamics and power

Systems and governance — institutional policy, approval pathways, and procurement practices

On who shapes the work: The people closest to a barrier are the most useful guides to finding it. Capacity building designed without input from the communities most affected by existing practices tends to produce changes that are visible on paper and marginal in experience.

Where Is Your Organisation in This Work?

This reflection is designed to surface honest questions about your organisation's current facilitation practice and identify where focused attention may be most productive. There are no right or wrong answers — choose the response that most accurately reflects your current situation. The reflection takes about two minutes.

2. How are participants' perspectives on access and inclusion gathered in your context?
